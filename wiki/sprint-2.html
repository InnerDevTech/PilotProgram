Status
Done
Changed from the plan. This sprint was going to be 16 infrared beams in a frame around the desk, read by an Arduino Mega. It is now the webcam plus Google’s MediaPipe, in software. Why: no IR parts to order or solder, nothing to knock out of alignment, no sunlight problem, and a position accurate to centimetres instead of to one 6 cm beam spacing. The cost is that the desk now needs calibrating — the beams were the coordinate system, and a camera is not — so the four-corner calibration that was going to be part of Sprint 3 has moved into this sprint.

User stories

  • As a user, my hand over the desk shows a glowing dot on the projection.
  • As a user, I calibrate once by clicking the four corners of the projection, and it stays calibrated until something gets bumped.
  • As a user, a hand held beside the desk does not put a dot on the projection.

The webcam is a Logitech C270. The scripts find it by name, so it does not matter what number Windows gives it — see “What actually happened”.

Expected outcome

By the end of Sprint 2 the webcam should be fixed above the desk looking down, with its position and the projector’s marked in tape. Clicking the four corners of the projection once writes calibration.json, and from then on the software can turn any camera pixel into a desk coordinate — two numbers from 0 to 1 across the projection, which is the one coordinate system every later sprint will use.

With that in place, MediaPipe finds the hand in each frame, three palm landmarks are averaged into a single point, the homography puts that point on the desk, and the page draws a glowing dot there. The dot should land within about 3 cm of your palm and be there roughly nine times out of ten.

Just as important is what it must not do. It must not appear on an empty desk, and it must not appear for a hand that is in the camera’s view but beside the projection. Two things handle that, and both are worth being able to explain: MediaPipe’s own confidence thresholds, and PresenceGate, which makes a hand prove itself over two frames before the dot appears and over six before it vanishes.

No objects and no physics yet — the dot is the whole deliverable.

Demo checklist

  • The calibration grid sits square on the projection.
  • The page shows "Hand sensor: connected".
  • The hand dot follows a hand from left to right and top to bottom.
  • An empty desk shows no dot for 5 minutes.
  • A hand held beside the desk shows no dot.
  • The test suite passes.

Definition of done

The hand dot works for 5 minutes with no false hands, on the real desk, in the room you will demo in.

Risk to watch: the camera or the projector gets bumped, and every dot lands in the wrong place with nothing on screen to say why. Mark both positions with tape and re-run calibrate_desk.py after any move. Second risk: your own arm shadowing the desk, or a hand that leaves the camera’s view before it leaves the desk — check the camera is high enough and genuinely sees the whole rectangle.

What actually happened

The hand dot works on the real desk: it follows a hand left to right and top to bottom, an empty desk stays empty, and the clock dims behind the dot while a hand is present. Every test case above passed, and the automated suite went from 34 tests before the sprint to 83.

What took longer than expected

Switching from IR beams to the webcam meant calibration had to happen in this sprint rather than Sprint 3, because without the beam grid nothing says where the projection is in the camera picture. MediaPipe 1.2.0 also no longer installs its hand model through pip, so it has to be downloaded separately (python bridge/hand_tracker.py --where-is-the-model).

Carried into Sprint 3: the camera is in a temporary position, which is why it sees the desk mirrored. Mounting it permanently above the desk is Sprint 3’s first job, and calibration must be re-run once it is there.
